## The short answer: count both, separately, the same way every week

Every hybrid church eventually has this meeting. Someone reports "we had 185 on Sunday," someone else asks "does that include online?", and twenty minutes later the room has invented three incompatible definitions of attendance. The way out is a simple counting policy:

1. **Count the room** with a consistent method at a consistent moment.
2. **Count the stream** as peak concurrent viewers during the live window.
3. **Report both numbers side by side, plus their sum** — one Total Reach, with the in-person and online halves always visible.
4. **Never change the method** — because the trend matters more than any single Sunday's total.

That's the whole policy. The rest of this article is why each piece matters and how the counting actually happens without adding work to anyone's Sunday.

## Why "separately" is the load-bearing word

The temptation is to pick one grand total and move on. But a single blended number hides exactly the stories leadership needs to see: in-room attendance dipping over the summer while online grows; online stagnating while the room recovers; a service type that reaches few people in person but travels far on the stream.

There's also an honesty problem with equivalence. A screen isn't a seat — one connection might be a college student alone or a family of five on a living-room TV. Rather than inventing a multiplier you can't defend, keep the units clean: *people counted in the room* and *screens watching live*. Both are real, both are measured, and neither pretends to be the other. When the numbers sit side by side every week, everyone learns to read them fluently — and the sum, [Total Reach](/service-metrics/), gives the one-line answer for the board report without erasing the detail.

And behind the online number are people worth counting well: the member who gave faithfully for fifty years and now worships from a recliner, the family that moved away, the traveling parent in a hotel, the not-yet-visitor quietly deciding whether to walk in. They aren't an asterisk on attendance. They're the congregation.

## Counting the room without the clipboard

Consistency in the room comes down to *method* and *moment*.

**Method:** WorshipMetrics gives you two, and they work together. The **seat-map counter** in Tech Manager is a saved map of your actual room — sections, rows, seats — that a volunteer taps on Sunday: tap a seat to toggle it, hold to assign a member or mark a first-time visitor. **Camera Auto-Count** goes further and removes the human arithmetic entirely: an RTSP camera or webcam looks at the room and the booth computer counts, on a schedule or on demand. Most churches run the camera for the main room and keep seat maps for the places no camera sees — the balcony, the cry room, the overflow.

**Moment:** the auto-counter's schedule is the quiet hero. A count taken twenty minutes in, every single week, is a measuring stick; a count taken "whenever the usher was free" is a guess. Pick a settled moment — not the opening song, not greeting time — and never change it. The full setup, including framing the camera and validating against manual counts, is in the [camera Auto-Count guide](/kb/articles/camera-auto-count-attendance-setup/).

If you run multiple services, each gets its own count attached to its own service — the 9:00 and the 11:00 stay distinct numbers instead of blurring into one Sunday total.

## Counting the stream without screenshots

The online half is even less work: connect your YouTube or Facebook broadcast and concurrent viewers are **sampled every minute** during the service, with the **peak and average** rolled into that day's attendance automatically when the broadcast ends. Streams through the WorshipMetrics player count the same way. No one screenshots a dashboard, and no one forgets.

Why peak concurrent rather than the platform's "views" number? Because views accumulate replays, partial watches, and repeat visits over days — it's a content metric, not an attendance metric. The full argument, and what the minute-by-minute curve reveals about when your online congregation actually shows up, is in [how churches know how many people watched the livestream](/kb/articles/how-many-watched-church-livestream/).

## What the policy unlocks: trends you can act on

Once both halves are measured consistently, Attendance Over Time turns the weekly numbers into the comparisons leaders actually ask for — in-person, virtual, and total by month, quarter, or year, broken out per service type. Did Easter beat last Easter? What did the sermon series do? Is the online congregation growing or quietly going stagnant? These stop being impressions and become lookups.

That's also when attendance data starts feeding ministry rather than just reporting. First-time visitors marked at the seat map or captured through [QR check-in](/kb/articles/qr-check-in-setup-church/) land in the directory where your [follow-up team](/church-guest-follow-up-system/) can actually reach them — counting, done well, is the front end of hospitality.
