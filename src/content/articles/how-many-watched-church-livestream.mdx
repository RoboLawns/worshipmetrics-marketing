## The short answer: count concurrent viewers during the live window, automatically

The honest measure of an online congregation is **how many screens were watching while the service was live** — and if your broadcast is connected to WorshipMetrics, that number takes care of itself. Concurrent viewers on a connected YouTube or Facebook broadcast are **sampled every minute**, during and after the service, and when the broadcast ends the **peak and average** roll into that day's attendance record automatically. Streams through the WorshipMetrics player count the same way. Nobody writes anything down.

That's the mechanics. The more interesting question is why the number your platform shows you by default — "views" — isn't the one to report to leadership, and what a trustworthy weekly measure actually looks like.

## Why "1,200 views" doesn't mean 1,200 worshipers

Every streaming platform reports views, and every platform means something slightly different by it. View counts accumulate over days and weeks; they mix live watchers with replay watchers; they include the person who watched for a few seconds while scrolling, and they can count the same person several times across devices and visits. None of that makes the number dishonest — it's just answering a different question. "Views" measures *content reach over time*. It doesn't measure *who worshiped with you on Sunday*.

Concurrent viewers — how many connections were watching at once, at a given minute — is the metric that behaves like attendance. It has a shape across the service (climbing through the first songs, peaking mid-sermon), it has a peak, and it has an average. When a leader asks "how many people were with us online?", peak concurrent is the defensible answer, with the average alongside it as the steadier week-to-week signal.

The minute-by-minute sampling matters more than it first appears. A single end-of-service number can't tell you *when* people joined or left; a per-minute curve can. If online viewers consistently arrive fifteen minutes late, that's a real insight about your online congregation's habits — and it's invisible in any single total.

## One record per service, next to the in-room count

The viewer numbers don't land in a silo. Each service's recap in [Service Metrics](/service-metrics/) pairs the online figures with the in-person count — from the seat-map counter or [camera Auto-Count](/kb/articles/camera-auto-count-attendance-setup/) — plus the broadcast comments that came in during the stream. Together they produce **Total Reach**: one number for everyone who worshiped with you, with the in-room and online halves always visible separately.

Keeping the halves separate is the part leaders come to rely on. A flat total can hide a real story — in-room dipping over the summer while online quietly grows, or the reverse. The shape of the congregation matters as much as its size, and you only see shape when the streams are counted distinctly. ## Consistency is what makes the number mean something

An attendance metric earns trust the same way a bathroom scale does: by measuring the same way every time. Automatic counting is what delivers that consistency — the sampling happens every minute of every broadcast, whether the tech booth had a calm morning or a chaotic one, whether the volunteer remembered or didn't.

That consistency is also what makes comparisons legitimate. Attendance Over Time charts in-person, virtual, and total by month, quarter, or year, with each service type broken out separately — so "Easter versus last Easter" and "is the online congregation growing or stagnant?" are answered from identically measured data, not from a mix of hand-recorded guesses and platform screenshots.

## What the numbers are ultimately for

It's worth ending where the counting should end: the people. The online congregation isn't an abstraction — it's the homebound member who never misses a Sunday, the family that moved three states away, and the not-yet-visitor quietly deciding whether your church is safe to walk into. Counting them accurately is the first step in treating them as the congregation they already are; connecting those attendance signals to [follow-up workflows](/church-visitor-follow-up-software/) is the second.
