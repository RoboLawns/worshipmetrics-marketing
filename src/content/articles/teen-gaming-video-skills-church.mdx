## The skills were never wasted

If you're the parent of a teenager whose natural habitat is a screen — the gamer, the streamer, the one who edits videos you never see posted — you've probably run the quiet math of worry: *so much time, and what's it for?* And if that same teenager seems to shrink at church, half-present, gifts invisible in a place built around singing and speaking, the worry doubles.

Here's a reframe from the people who run church production booths: **those hours have been job training.** The gamer has built live reflexes and calm under pressure. The streamer understands an audience on the other side of a screen. The phone photographer has been practicing framing for years. The playlist obsessive has ears most adults never develop. These aren't distractions from the person your teen is becoming — they're early drafts of gifts. What's been missing isn't discipline. It's a *destination*.

## The destination is thirty feet from the back pew

Every Sunday, your church produces a live broadcast, a soundtrack, a lyrics presentation, and a recording — real production, with real seats: [camera](/av-team/camera-operator/), [lyrics](/av-team/lyrics-operator/), [audio](/av-team/audio-engineer/), [the stream](/av-team/stream-operator/), [recording and clips](/av-team/recording-archivist/). Most of those seats are understaffed in most churches, filled by one tired adult who would be *overjoyed* to train a teenager who already halfway knows this world.

And here's what happens to a screen-native kid in one of those seats — the thing parents tell us they didn't expect. The isolation flips. The same skills that kept them alone in their room put them on a *crew*: headsets, shared work, an adult mentor who thinks their instincts are impressive, and a whole congregation depending on them by name. Researchers who study gaming will tell you much of its pull is belonging — a community with a shared ritual. A booth crew is that, except the ritual is worship and the community can see them grow.

## What it looks like when it's done well

You're right to ask whether this is safe and supported rather than a kid thrown at a mixing board. The healthy pattern, which you can ask any church about directly:

- **One seat, one clear job** — not "help with tech." A defined role, [matched to their age and wiring](/tools/church-av-seat-finder/).
- **A mentor, present or watching** — beside them at first, and able to see their actual controls live even from home as they grow. Mistakes surface in preview, where nobody but the mentor sees them.
- **A safety net under everything** — modern systems catch the routine failures automatically, so a new volunteer genuinely [can't sink Sunday](/kb/articles/students-run-church-livestream-safely/).
- **Recognition** — crews get thanked; students get named. Belonging gets sealed out loud.

If your church runs something like an [AV Club](/av-club/), all of this is already structured. If it doesn't, forwarding [the starter guide](/kb/articles/start-church-av-club-vmix/) to your youth pastor or tech director is genuinely how several of these clubs have begun — and [the printable one-page proposal](/av-club/proposal/) means your suggestion arrives with its homework already done.

## The conversation to have this week

Skip the screen-time framing entirely — this isn't a trade or a bribe. Try the invitation the booth would give them: *"You know how you're good at [the actual thing — streaming, video, sound]? The church actually needs that. Want to try one Sunday, sitting next to the person who runs it?"*

Specific. Honoring. One Sunday. Teenagers can refuse a program, but very few can fully refuse being *needed* for something they're already good at.

And a season from now, you may get the moment other parents of booth kids describe: watching the congregation sing, and realizing the reason the words are on the screen — on time, every time — is your kid, at the back, wearing a headset, exactly where they belong.
