## The question behind the question

When a church starts shopping for a live streaming platform, the stated question is usually "how do we get our service onto the internet?" The real question is bigger: **who is responsible for Sunday going well online?**

A streaming platform is not just a pipe. It's the thing your online congregation experiences directly. When it works, nobody thinks about it. When it fails at 9:07 on a Sunday morning, it's the only thing anyone thinks about.

So evaluate platforms against the whole job, not just the pipe.

## The five jobs a streaming platform should do

### 1. Be the destination

The healthiest streaming architecture for a volunteer-run booth is **one feed out**. Your encoder — vMix, OBS, or a hardware box — points at one ingest, once. Everything downstream happens on the platform side: destinations, recordings, monitoring.

If your operator has to manage separate stream keys for YouTube and Facebook, re-enter settings after every update, or "just quickly check" three dashboards during the prelude, your architecture is fragile. Fragile architectures fail on the weeks your most experienced volunteer is out.

### 2. Fan out to every platform that matters

Multi-destination streaming (restreaming) means your one feed reaches YouTube, Facebook, your website embed, and any custom RTMP destination simultaneously. Two things to check:

- **Adding a destination should be a click, not a project.** If trying a new platform requires touching the encoder, your team won't experiment.
- **Your website should get a clean, unbranded player.** Your church's site shouldn't push people to a third-party platform to watch.

For a deeper look at the multi-platform decision itself — which platforms are worth streaming to and why — see our guide to [streaming to YouTube, Facebook and more simultaneously](/kb/articles/multistream-youtube-facebook-church/).

### 3. Record everything, automatically

If your platform doesn't automatically capture every service into a recordings library, you are one "forgot to hit record" away from losing a baptism, a testimony, or the sermon your pastor wanted to reference.

The recordings library is also where the second life of your service starts: trimming the full service, cutting the sermon for the podcast, and handing highlights to a clipping workflow. See [clipping sermons for social media](/kb/articles/clipping-sermons-social-media/) for how churches turn one service into a week of content.

### 4. Watch its own health

The most common way churches discover a stream failure is a text message from someone watching at home. That's backwards.

A real platform monitors bitrate, dropped frames, and encoder load in real time — and **alerts you the moment quality degrades**, not after the service ends. If a platform can't tell you the stream is struggling before your congregation can, it's leaving the most stressful part of the job to chance.

### 5. Answer the phone

Here is the criterion almost nobody puts on the comparison spreadsheet, and it's the one that matters most in an actual crisis: **when the stream breaks on Sunday morning, can you reach a human who understands church production?**

Most streaming vendors offer email tickets with business-hours response. Sunday morning is not business hours. Ask every vendor you evaluate one question: *"It's Sunday, 9:07am, and my stream is down. What happens when I call you?"* The answer tells you everything.
