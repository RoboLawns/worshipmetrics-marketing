## Why PoE deserves ten minutes of your attention

Walk through a modern church AV rig and count the devices that get their power from an Ethernet cable: PTZ cameras on the back wall, Wi-Fi access points over the lobby, a touch panel at the tech booth, maybe an intercom beltpack station or a Dante wall plate. Power over Ethernet is one of the best things to happen to church installs — one cable carries both signal and power, so a camera can live thirty feet up a wall with no electrician involved.

It is also one of the most common sources of mystery failures. The classic symptoms all trace back to power, not networking:

- A PTZ camera that works on some switch ports but not others
- A camera that reboots when a nearby access point powers up
- A touch panel that boots, shows its logo, then dies halfway through startup
- An entire bank of devices that browns out when one more thing is plugged in

Every one of these is a PoE class or power budget problem, and every one of them is preventable with a little arithmetic. This article covers the three PoE standards, the difference between a per-port cap and a chassis budget, and how to size a switch for a real church rig.

## The three standards: 802.3af, 802.3at, 802.3bt

PoE is standardized by the IEEE, and the three generations you will meet in the wild are usually marketed as PoE, PoE+, and PoE++ (or "4PPoE").

**802.3af (Type 1, "PoE")** — the original 2003 standard. The switch can supply up to 15.4 W at the port, of which about 12.95 W is guaranteed to reach the device after cable losses. Plenty for VoIP phones, small IP cameras, and simple wall panels.

**802.3at (Type 2, "PoE+")** — the 2009 revision. Up to 30 W at the port, roughly 25.5 W delivered. This is the workhorse class for church AV: most PTZ cameras, most modern Wi-Fi access points, and most touch panels are PoE+ devices.

**802.3bt (Types 3 and 4, "PoE++")** — the 2018 standard, which delivers power over all four wire pairs instead of two. Type 3 supplies up to 60 W at the port (about 51 W delivered); Type 4 goes to 90 W at the port (about 71 W delivered). You will see 802.3bt on larger PTZ cameras with big zoom blocks and heaters, video conferencing bars, LED fixtures, and small computers.

Within those standards, devices negotiate a **class** — a number from 0 to 8 that tells the switch how much power to set aside:

| Class | Standard | Max power at switch port | Max power at device |
|-------|----------|--------------------------|---------------------|
| 0–3 | 802.3af | up to 15.4 W | up to 12.95 W |
| 4 | 802.3at | 30 W | 25.5 W |
| 5 | 802.3bt Type 3 | 45 W | 40 W |
| 6 | 802.3bt Type 3 | 60 W | 51 W |
| 7 | 802.3bt Type 4 | 75 W | 62 W |
| 8 | 802.3bt Type 4 | 90 W | 71.3 W |

Two practical notes on that table. First, the standards are backward compatible: an 802.3bt switch will happily power an 802.3af phone, and the negotiation handles the details. Second, beware of **passive PoE**, which is not part of any of these standards. Some older wireless gear ships with passive 24 V injectors that skip negotiation entirely and just put voltage on the wire. Passive PoE and standards-based PoE do not mix — plugging the wrong combination together can damage equipment — so keep passive-powered gear on its own clearly labeled injectors.

## Per-port caps vs. the chassis budget

Here is the trap that catches most teams. A switch has **two separate limits**, and the spec sheet headline usually only tells you one of them.

The **per-port cap** is the maximum any single port can deliver — this is what "PoE+ switch" tells you. Every port on a 802.3at switch can do up to 30 W.

The **chassis budget** (or PoE budget) is the total the switch's internal power supply can deliver across *all* ports at once. And this is where the arithmetic bites: a "16-port PoE+ switch" with a 120 W budget cannot come close to powering sixteen 30 W loads. Sixteen ports times 30 W would be 480 W; the switch can actually hand out 120 W total, however you slice it.

There is one more wrinkle: many switches budget by **allocation, not measured draw**. When a Class 4 camera negotiates, the switch may reserve the full 30 W for that port even if the camera only pulls 14 W sitting still. That is conservative by design — the camera is allowed to spike to its class limit whenever it wants, such as when the zoom motor and IR illuminator kick in together. Switches that support LLDP power negotiation can allocate more precisely, but for planning purposes you should always budget by class allocation, not by the wattage you see on a meter during a quiet Tuesday test.

When the budget runs out, switches shed load — usually by refusing power to the lowest-priority port, which on many models simply means the highest port number. This is why "the camera worked until we added the new access point" is such a common story: the AP did not break anything, it just pushed the chassis past its budget and something had to lose.

## Sizing a switch for a real church rig

Let's size a production switch for a typical mid-size sanctuary. The method is simple: list every powered device, find its PoE class, sum the **allocations**, and add headroom.

| Device | Qty | Class | Allocation each | Subtotal |
|--------|-----|-------|-----------------|----------|
| PTZ cameras | 3 | Class 4 (PoE+) | 30 W | 90 W |
| Wi-Fi access points | 2 | Class 4 (PoE+) | 30 W | 60 W |
| Touch panels | 2 | Class 3 (PoE) | 15.4 W | 30.8 W |
| Intercom station | 1 | Class 2 (PoE) | 7 W | 7 W |
| **Total allocation** | | | | **~188 W** |

A 16-port PoE+ switch with a 150 W budget has plenty of *ports* for this rig — and not enough *power*. Eight powered devices at 188 W of allocation needs a switch with at least a 250 W budget once you add sensible headroom.

Why headroom? Three reasons:

1. **Cold-boot inrush.** On Sunday morning everything powers up at once, and simultaneous startup draw is the worst case the budget ever sees.
2. **The next device.** Churches add gear. A rig sized at 95% of budget fails the first time someone adds a confidence-monitor player or one more AP.
3. **Class creep.** Replacement devices tend to want more power than the ones they replace — the Wi-Fi 6E successor to your current AP may negotiate Class 5 or 6.

A 20–30% margin over total allocation is a good target. And a few cabling notes while you are planning: Cat5e handles 802.3af and 802.3at fine at any standard run length, but for 802.3bt loads — especially many of them bundled together in a conduit — Cat6a's heavier conductors dissipate heat better and are worth specifying. Avoid powering anything through long chains of thin patch cables, couplers, or old punch-down blocks; PoE faults love high-resistance joints.

Finally, resist the temptation to hang a small unmanaged desktop switch off a wall port to "split" a PoE feed. Most cheap switches neither pass through nor regenerate PoE, and the ones that do add another unbudgeted, undocumented power domain to your building. If a room needs three powered devices, it needs three runs to a real switch — your future self troubleshooting at 7:45 a.m. will thank you. ## Write it down before it bites you

The rigs that avoid PoE surprises all share one habit: the power story is documented, not remembered. For every powered device, record:

- **The PoE class it negotiates** (from the spec sheet, not a guess)
- **Which switch and port feeds it** — so "camera 2 is switch A port 7" is written down, not tribal knowledge
- **The switch's total budget and current allocation** — a one-line running total per switch
- **Any passive injectors or midspans** — clearly flagged, since they live outside the switch budget entirely

This matters most for the devices that make Sunday happen. A PTZ camera that browns out mid-service looks exactly like a camera failure, and teams have swapped healthy cameras over what was really a budget problem two racks away.
