## The short answer: yes — a QR code and a browser are the whole system

The app-download requirement is where church check-in systems go to die. Asking a first-time guest — the person you most want to capture — to install an app, create an account, and verify an email before saying "I was here" is asking for thirty minutes of commitment in exchange for thirty seconds of data. Almost nobody does it.

QR check-in removes every one of those steps. A poster in the lobby carries a code; an attender scans it with their phone's camera; a **mobile web page opens in the browser** — no app, no account — they type their name and tap check in. The record lands directly in your directory with attendance logged at the same moment, and nobody in the office retypes anything on Monday. That last part matters as much as the guest experience: every clipboard and connect card dies in transcription, where names get misspelled and the same person enters the database three times.

Here's how the pieces fit, including the two questions every team asks next: *what about people who won't scan?* and *how does this not create duplicate records?*

## One poster, or one code per service

You get two flavors of code, and the rule of thumb is simple: **permanent signage gets the org-wide code; anything date-specific gets a per-service code.**

The **org-wide code** points to your church's own check-in page, so the framed lobby poster you print in August still works in December. **Per-service codes** tie a scan to a particular gathering — a 9:00 code and an 11:00 code, or a dedicated code for the midweek study — so attendance attributes cleanly. And when an event needs reservations rather than door check-in (Christmas Eve, a limited-seating class), each service can enable a public registration link with its own print-ready QR code and capacity limits.

The codes generate print-ready; the physical-world tips that make scans reliable — sizing for distance, matte over glossy, testing under actual lobby lights — are in the full [QR check-in setup guide](/kb/articles/qr-check-in-setup-church/).

## The welcome-desk tablet covers everyone else

Some people won't scan — no smartphone, a dead battery, or simply a preference for talking to a human. That's the welcome-desk tablet's job: a greeter opens the same check-in page and enters a name and email on the attender's behalf. The matching logic is identical either way, so a regular checked in by a volunteer still lands on their existing record.

## Why this doesn't wreck your directory

Self-service data entry is only as good as its matching, so every incoming check-in tries to match an existing person **before** anyone new is created, strongest signal first: **email**, then **phone**, then **exact name plus birthday**. Name alone is never enough — your church almost certainly has two people who share one — but name and birthday together is a reliable fingerprint. The one habit worth coaching from the lobby: gently encourage email at check-in, because consistent email keeps one person on one profile forever.

When no tier matches, the system concludes this is a genuine first-timer and creates them as a **Guest** — with attendance recorded, a real timestamp, and a place in the directory your hospitality team can filter to "Guests added this month." Because people changes sync live, the office sees new check-ins appear within moments; by the time the sermon starts, someone already knows who's new today. That's the pastoral payoff: the nervous first-Sunday guest becomes a real record for [follow-up](/church-visitor-follow-up-software/), not a half-legible connect card.

The remaining edge case — the attender who used a work email in March and a personal one in June — is what the Monday duplicate-review queue is for: likely pairs flagged, a human makes the merge-or-dismiss call, and decisions persist so a dismissed pair never nags again. Five minutes a week keeps the directory clean; the public endpoints are rate-limited (300 submissions per 10 minutes per IP) so a bot can't flood it in the first place.
