## The clubhouse already exists — it's your booth

Most churches that want young people involved in tech start with a vague announcement: *"see the tech team if you're interested."* Nobody comes, and the conclusion gets drawn that kids aren't interested. The truth is usually the opposite — the students who spend their evenings editing videos, running Discord streams, and obsessing over game audio are *deeply* interested in exactly what your booth does. What's missing isn't interest. It's a **structure with a door on it**.

That structure is an AV Club: a named group with a regular rhythm, defined seats, a visible ladder, and adult mentors — and if your church runs vMix, the clubhouse is already built. A capable production system is the single best recruiting asset a church tech ministry can have, because it's *real*. Students can tell the difference between being given a job and being given a toy. Here's the launch plan, step by step.

## Step 1: Recruit where interest already exists

Skip the pulpit announcement (for now). Go directly to where the interest already lives: youth group leaders, homeschool families, senior-high small groups — and by name, the students who already like video, audio, design, streaming, or gaming. The invitation that works isn't "we need help." It's *"we have a real production system, and we'll teach you to run it."*

Aim for two or three students to start. A small club with a real rhythm beats a big signup list that meets twice and evaporates. And plan for the second wave to recruit itself — the first students' friends will follow once there's something real to join.

## Step 2: One seat, one clear job

The founding rule of the club, borrowed from [how the whole ecosystem works](/kb/articles/you-already-have-vmix/): **every member owns one seat**. Not "helps with tech" — owns the lyrics, or one camera, or the [seat-map tablet](/kb/articles/church-check-in-without-an-app/), or the [clips queue](/kb/articles/sunday-sermon-instagram-before-monday/) that turns Sunday's sermon into Monday's Reels. Match the seat to age and maturity: a 14-year-old can run one camera well; the tablet and clips seats have even lower floors; the vMix chair itself is the summit, not the entrance.

One seat per person does three jobs at once: training stays small and specific ("here is your job" beats "here is everything"), the room genuinely depends on each student (which is where belonging comes from), and the [safety story stays honest](/kb/articles/students-run-church-livestream-safely/) — nobody holds more responsibility than they're ready for.

## Step 3: Let the system do the supervising

The reason an AV Club is *practical* now, in a way it wasn't a decade ago, is that the ecosystem around your vMix machine carries most of the supervision load:

- **A mentor sees everything from anywhere.** The [cloud panel](/kb/articles/multiple-operators-vmix-church/) mirrors preview, program, every input, and the audio meters to any device — so coaching happens over a shoulder or over a text, and a student's choice is visible in preview before it ever hits air.
- **The routine failures fix themselves.** [Self-healing](/kb/articles/tech-manager-self-healing-explained/) restarts dropped streams and stopped recordings, so a student's Sunday doesn't hinge on crisis reflexes nobody should expect them to have yet.
- **Everything is logged.** Access lists say who can touch what; the support history says who did what. Parents and boards get an audit trail, not assurances.
- **The debrief has data.** The [stream health score](/kb/articles/reading-your-stream-health-score/) turns each service into a teachable timeline — and a string of healthy scores into visible proof the students are doing it well. Read a month of them together at club meetings; it teaches failure patterns faster than any hypothetical.

## Step 4: Build the ladder in public

A club needs somewhere to go. Make the progression explicit and visible: tablet → lyrics → camera → stream monitor → the vMix chair — and beyond it, the club's own leadership: the 17-year-old who trains the 14-year-old, the student who owns the [Signal Map](/system-documentation/) and keeps the documentation true, the one who runs the [scheduling matrix](/kb/articles/volunteer-scheduling-without-text-scramble/) for the whole rotation. Older students mentoring younger ones is the moment the club stops consuming the tech director's energy and starts generating its own.

Then celebrate in public. Thank the students from stage sometimes; name who ran cameras on the big Sunday. Recognition is what turns a task into belonging — and belonging is the actual product here, as the next post in this series argues: [the tech booth is a youth ministry](/kb/articles/tech-booth-youth-ministry/).

## Step 5: Give it a rhythm

The minimum viable club: a monthly meeting (pizza, one skill taught, one month of health reports reviewed, one round of recognition) plus serving rotations staffed [a month at a time](/kb/articles/scheduling-volunteers-month-at-a-time/) so students and parents can plan. Blockouts let students mark exam weeks themselves; day-before reminders mean nobody's mom has to remember.

That's the whole launch: real system, real seats, real ladder, real rhythm.
