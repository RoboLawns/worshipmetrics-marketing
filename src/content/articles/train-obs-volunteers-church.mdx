## The short answer: they know the software — teach them the Sunday

Church tech training has always budgeted most of its hours for the tool: which button, which panel, which setting. For OBS booths recruiting from [the generation that grew up on OBS](/kb/articles/youth-group-already-knows-obs/), that budget is obsolete. The student who streams on weeknights already knows scenes, sources, and going live — often more fluently than the trainer. What they *don't* know is your church's Sunday: the service flow, the cue points, what matters pastorally, what to do at 10:22 when something blinks red.

So the modern OBS training method inverts the old one: **assume software fluency, teach the service, and let the system carry the risk while judgment develops.** Four practices make it work — and they work for your less-fluent adult recruits too, just with one extra hour on the front end.

## Practice 1: one seat, one hour, one month

Nobody trains for "the booth." You train one person for **one seat** — the stream output, a camera, the monitors, [the recording](/kb/articles/obs-recording-unplayable-after-crash/) — with one clear job description. For a fluent recruit, the teaching hour is mostly translation: *here's our scene collection, here's the service order, here's what we never touch on Sundays* ([the settings baseline](/kb/articles/obs-settings-changed-between-sundays/) does a lot of quiet work here). Then a month of supervised Sundays turns knowledge into ownership. The [ladder](/kb/articles/you-already-have-obs/) — seat to seat, eventually to training others — takes care of ambition.

## Practice 2: rehearse for real, in draft

The trainee's fear is polluting something; remove it structurally. Every run of a service plan in WorshipMetrics is either a **draft** (rehearsal, walkthrough, tech night) or **committed** (the real thing) — and [only committed runs enter your history](/kb/articles/live-run-test-vs-real-service/). So Thursday night, the trainee runs the *entire* Sunday — same scenes, same slides, same stream-start ritual — as many times as their nerves need, and the records stay pristine. By Sunday, nothing is new except the congregation.

## Practice 3: the mentor watches the real controls — from anywhere

This is the practice that changed everything, and it's why training no longer requires the veteran's physical Sunday. The trainee sits at OBS; the mentor watches the *same live state* — what's streaming, what's recording, current scene, [the machine's vitals](/kb/articles/is-obs-stream-actually-working/) — [from the next chair or their living room](/kb/articles/obs-help-from-home-sunday/), able to coach by headset or text, and able to switch scenes or manage the stream remotely if a moment demands it. Every remote action is logged, so the training record writes itself.

And underneath both of them, [self-healing](/kb/articles/tech-manager-self-healing-explained/) holds the floor: dropped streams restart, stopped recordings recover. Tell the trainee this on day one — *"you cannot sink Sunday"* — because learners who know the floor holds take the small risks that build real skill. It's the same architecture that makes [student operators safe](/kb/articles/students-run-church-livestream-safely/), applied to trainees of any age.

## Practice 4: debrief from the timeline

Feedback from memory is vague and bruising; feedback from telemetry is precise and kind. After each training Sunday, open the [health report](/kb/articles/reading-your-stream-health-score/) together: the 0-100 score, each event timestamped, the settings the stream ran with. The conversation becomes *"here's the reconnect at 10:22 — what did you notice?"* — evidence, not opinion. A month of reports teaches failure patterns faster than a year of hypotheticals, and a stack of clean ones is how a trainee *knows* they've arrived, no pep talk required.

## What the trainer gets back

Notice the labor math: the old way, training cost the veteran every Sunday for months — which is [why it never happened](/kb/articles/church-tech-burnout-cycle/) and why [more OBS volunteers](/kb/articles/get-obs-volunteers-church/) never materialized. The new way costs one teaching hour, a few remotely-supervised services, and Monday coffee over a report. Training stops competing with the trainer's survival — and somewhere around week six, the student you trained starts explaining the scene collection to the *next* recruit, which is the moment your booth stops having a training problem forever.
