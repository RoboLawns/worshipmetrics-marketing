## The short answer: the booth should report itself — to everyone responsible

Every church with a livestream knows the blind-spot feeling. The pastor's spouse texts *"is the stream down?"* and nobody knows without calling the booth. The youth minister wonders how the student volunteer is doing and has no way to check short of driving over. The AV lead spends Sunday *not present* but *not at rest*, because the only monitoring system is being physically in the room.

OBS, on its own, can't fix this — it's an encoder, not a witness. The fix is connecting it: [Tech Manager](/tech-manager/) sits beside OBS on the booth machine and sends **telemetry heartbeats continuously** — a light beat every 15 seconds, a full snapshot every 60 — carrying stream state, recording state, encoder performance, and the machine's own vitals to the cloud. From that moment, "is it actually working?" has a live answer, on any authorized phone, for **everyone who should be able to see**: the AV lead, the video producer at home, the youth minister whose student is at the desk, the leadership that approved the whole arrangement.

That last sentence is the quiet revolution, so let's take it seriously.

## Honest status, engineered for trust

Monitoring is only useful if people believe it, so the design is deliberately honest. The **online badge flips after 90 seconds** of missed heartbeats — fast enough to be truthful, and cheap to glance at. The **offline alert email waits five minutes** of confirmed silence — because an email interrupts someone's morning, it fires only when the outage is real. And the checking runs **server-side, around the clock**: detection never depends on anyone having a dashboard open. [The exact thresholds are published](/kb/articles/pulse-alert-thresholds-reference/), which is itself the point — when the system says *healthy*, everyone from the booth to the board can trust the word.

Alerts complete the loop for the moments nobody's looking: a stream that leaves the live state gets [a drop alert to owners, admins, and operators automatically](/kb/articles/church-stream-drop-phone-alert/), with push notifications for any phone that's opted in, and a 90-day feed so "has this happened before?" is a lookup. Meanwhile [self-healing](/kb/articles/tech-manager-self-healing-explained/) is already restarting the dropped stream after 30 seconds down — so a good number of alerts arrive reading *resolved*.

## After the service: the report nobody has to compile

Live status answers "is it working *now*?" The [health report](/kb/articles/reading-your-stream-health-score/) answers the Monday question — *did it actually work?* — with a 0-100 score per service, every deduction timestamped, and the stream's settings captured. A pastor reads the number; the AV lead reads the timeline; a [trainee reads it as curriculum](/kb/articles/train-obs-volunteers-church/). One artifact, every audience, zero compiling.

## Why this is the youth ministry's favorite dashboard

Here's the angle most monitoring articles miss, and the reason this question belongs in the same conversation as [your youth group already knowing OBS](/kb/articles/youth-group-already-knows-obs/): **visibility is what converts "risky" into "apprenticed."** The reason churches hesitated to [hand students real OBS access](/kb/articles/students-run-church-livestream-safely/) was never doubt about their fluency — it was that nobody could *see* what was happening without standing behind them, which made every delegated Sunday an act of blind faith.

Cloud monitoring ends the blindness for every responsible party at once. The youth minister glances at a phone mid-service and sees their student's stream running clean. The AV lead confirms the recording from a lake house. Leadership sees a month of healthy scores attached to a sixteen-year-old's name, and the [next AV Club proposal](/av-club/proposal/) sails through on evidence. The student, meanwhile, gets the dignity every apprentice deserves: real responsibility, really witnessed — and a documented track record that no "good job, buddy" could ever match.
