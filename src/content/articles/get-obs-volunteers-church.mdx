## The short answer: recruit the people who already own the software

"We need volunteers for the livestream" fails as an ask for a reason this series [has covered before](/kb/articles/recruit-church-tech-volunteers/): it asks *anyone* for *everything*, and humble people rule themselves out. But for OBS booths specifically, there's a second, stranger failure underneath it — **churches recruit as if OBS fluency is rare, in the middle of the most OBS-fluent generation that has ever existed.**

Run the numbers on your own congregation: [85% of teens play video games](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2024/05/09/teens-and-video-games-today/), and the streaming culture around gaming runs on OBS — the same free software in your booth. The kid who streams to eleven Twitch viewers on weeknights has live-production hours most of your adult candidates will never accumulate. [Your youth group already knows OBS](/kb/articles/youth-group-already-knows-obs/); the recruiting problem is mostly that nobody's asked them *as if that mattered*.

So here's the OBS-specific recruiting playbook, in four moves.

## Move 1: get names from the youth pastor, not hands from the pulpit

The announcement filters for confidence; the invitation filters for fit. Ask your youth pastor one question: *"which students stream, game seriously, or edit video?"* — then invite those students **by name, citing the thing they're already good at**: *"I've seen you stream. We need exactly that on Sunday mornings — want to see the booth?"* For a generation used to being told their hobby is a waste, being recruited *because of it* lands with surprising force. (Parents feel it too — there's a [whole post for them](/kb/articles/teen-gaming-video-skills-church/) worth forwarding with the invitation.)

Don't skip the adults, but recruit them the same way: the photographer, the podcast listener, the detail-lover — [matched to a seat](/tools/church-av-seat-finder/), not thrown at "tech."

## Move 2: offer a seat, not the booth

OBS booths tempt churches into recruiting for the whole cockpit because one person has always run it. Resist. The winning offer is **one seat with one clear job**: run the stream output, own the cameras, watch the monitors, [manage the recording](/kb/articles/obs-recording-unplayable-after-crash/). One seat takes an hour to teach — [less when the recruit arrives half-trained](/kb/articles/train-obs-volunteers-church/) — and a month to own, and the seats form a ladder that gives a student somewhere to grow. That structure is what [one OBS system with a team around it](/kb/articles/you-already-have-obs/) actually looks like.

## Move 3: say the safety net out loud — because now there is one

The unspoken reason churches under-recruit for OBS: handing a stranger the livestream used to be genuinely risky, and everyone knew it. Name what's changed, to the recruit *and* to the leaders who must approve them:

- **The routine failures fix themselves.** A dropped stream restarts after 30 seconds; a stopped recording restarts on detection — [the veteran reflexes are built in](/kb/articles/tech-manager-self-healing-explained/), so a rookie can't lose Sunday to a blip.
- **The responsible adults can see.** Cloud-connected OBS means the AV lead, the youth minister, and anyone leadership designates [watches the booth's live state from anywhere](/kb/articles/is-obs-stream-actually-working/) — and a mentor can [step in remotely](/kb/articles/obs-help-from-home-sunday/) mid-service if needed.
- **Everything is on the record.** Remote actions are logged, and every service produces a [health report](/kb/articles/reading-your-stream-health-score/) — so trust is built on data, not vibes.

That's the sentence that unlocks both the volunteer's yes and the board's: *new hands on a watched surface.*

## Move 4: make staying easy

Recruiting gets them in; logistics keep them. Month-ahead invites with [one-tap responses and self-serve blockouts](/kb/articles/volunteer-scheduling-without-text-scramble/) respect a student's exam weeks and a parent's calendar; day-before reminders send themselves; public thanks from the stage seals the belonging. Retention was never mysterious — people stay where they're organized, needed, and seen.

## Who this rescues

Behind every "how do we get OBS volunteers" search is usually one specific exhausted person: the video producer who's carried the stream alone for years. This playbook is their exit from [the burnout cycle](/kb/articles/church-tech-burnout-cycle/) — not by finding one heroic replacement, but by surrounding the booth they built with the [generation that grew up speaking its language](/kb/articles/youth-group-already-knows-obs/). The volunteers exist. They're in the building on Wednesdays. Ask them properly, seat them safely, and give your producer back their Sundays.
