## The relief hiding in the problem

Most churches don't have a social media person. Leaders read the "post Reels consistently" advice, look around for someone to do it, find no one, and file the whole idea under *someday*. If that's your church, here's the reframe that changes everything: **you were looking for the wrong person.**

The reason "social media volunteer" felt like a unicorn is that the role used to require a stack of skills — video editing, an eye for moments, transcription patience, formatting know-how, *and* the platform sense to post it well. That's a professional, and professionals cost money churches don't have. But when [the software finds and cuts the moments for you](/kb/articles/how-ai-finds-sermon-moments/), that whole stack collapses to one skill: **taste.** And taste is common, cheap, and trainable in an afternoon.

## Recruit for taste, not editing

Stop looking for someone who "does video." Start looking for someone who has a *feel* — for a moment that lands, for your church's voice, for what a friend would actually stop scrolling to watch. Two profiles fill this role beautifully:

- **A student.** The [generation that already lives in short video](/kb/articles/youth-group-already-knows-obs/) thinks in Reels natively — they don't need to be taught the format because it's their first language. Clip review is also a perfect [entry seat on the tech ministry ladder](/kb/articles/serving-opportunities-teens-without-stage/): real responsibility, low technical floor, done from a phone.
- **A detail-oriented parent or member.** Someone who wants a flexible, from-home way to serve — the clip review happens on their own schedule, on their couch, in the margins of a week. It's one of the few church jobs with no Sunday-morning presence required.

The recruiting ask writes itself, and it's [the specific, named invitation that actually works](/kb/articles/recruit-church-tech-volunteers/): *"You've got a great eye for this stuff — would you pick our two best moments from Sunday each week? The software already cuts them; you just choose."*

## The afternoon training session

Because the software carries the craft, training is genuinely one sitting:

1. **Show them the ranked list.** Open [Worship Clips](/solutions/worship-clips/) and walk through a real service's AI-found moments — the shareability scores, the categories, the suggested hooks. The concept lands in five minutes: *the computer already watched the service and flagged the good parts.*
2. **Teach the one judgment call.** Which moment fits *our* church? Talk through two or three examples — why this sermon point travels, why that inside-joke moment won't land with a newcomer. This is the entire skill, and it's a conversation, not a curriculum.
3. **Do one together.** Pick a moment, tweak the caption from the draft, download the vertical render, post it. Watch them realize it took four minutes.
4. **Set the rhythm.** Agree on a cadence they can sustain — two clips a week beats ten this week and none after. Point them at [the volunteer's workflow guide](/kb/articles/social-media-volunteer-church-reels/) for reference and let them run.

That's it. No editing software to master, no formatting to learn, no transcription to babysit.

## Why this role sticks when others don't

Volunteer roles fail when they're vague, heavy, or lonely. Clip review is the opposite: it's **specific** (pick and post), **light** (a couple hours, from anywhere), and **visibly fruitful** — the volunteer watches their pick get views, likes, and the occasional "I saw your church's video!" That feedback loop is unusually motivating, which is why clip volunteers tend to *stay*. And it scales into a real ministry seat: today they pick moments, next quarter they're thinking about campaigns and [helping the church reach people it never would have](/kb/articles/pastors-grow-church-with-reels/).

The person you needed was never a video professional. It was someone with good instincts and two spare hours — and that person is already in your congregation. The software made the rest of the job disappear; you just have to ask them.
